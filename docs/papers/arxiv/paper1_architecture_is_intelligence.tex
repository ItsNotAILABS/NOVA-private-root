\begin{abstract}
As autonomous AI agents gain the ability to generate, compose, and deploy new
capabilities without human supervision, the question of \emph{who authored what}
becomes both technically and legally urgent.  Existing approaches treat
attribution as a contractual or metadata overlay — a label attached to a
capability after the fact, vulnerable to modification, deletion, or dispute.
We propose \emph{structural attribution}: a framework in which authorship is not
a label but a \emph{type-system invariant} encoded at capability genesis and
enforced by the runtime.  We formalise the Structural Attribution Type (SAT),
prove three invariants — \emph{genesis immutability}, \emph{compositional
transitivity}, and \emph{third-party verifiability} — and derive the
\emph{Attribution DAG}, a directed acyclic graph that traces the complete
authorship lineage of any composed capability.  We then show that a network of
AI agents operating under SAT achieves a property we call \emph{Attribution
Closure}: no agent can produce a capability without leaving an irrevocable,
machine-verifiable authorship record.  We discuss implications for AI
intellectual property law, open-source AI ecosystems, and the design of
multi-agent systems where capabilities are bought, sold, and composed at
autonomous-agent speed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The economics of software capability are changing faster than the legal and
technical infrastructure that governs them.  When a human engineer writes a
function, attribution is straightforward: the commit log, the copyright header,
and the employment contract together establish who made what.  When an
autonomous AI agent generates a capability — invokes a tool, produces an
algorithm, synthesises a new module from existing ones — none of those three
mechanisms apply reliably.  The commit log may not exist; the copyright header
may be missing or incorrect; the employment contract does not contemplate machine
authorship.

The result is what we call the \emph{Attribution Vacuum}: a growing space of
AI-generated capabilities for which authorship is contested, unresolved, or
simply unrecorded.  As AI agents proliferate and compose each other's outputs,
the vacuum grows faster than any contractual or metadata-based solution can
fill it.

This paper argues that the Attribution Vacuum is not a legal problem with a
legal solution.  It is an \emph{architectural problem with an architectural
solution}: authorship should be a \emph{type}, not a tag.  When authorship is
a type-system invariant — encoded at the moment a capability is created and
enforced by the runtime — it cannot be removed, forged, or disputed, because
it is not a claim about the capability; it is a \emph{structural property} of
the capability itself.

\paragraph{Contributions.}  We make four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item We define the \emph{Structural Attribution Type} (SAT) and specify its
    formal semantics, including what operations may and may not affect an
    attribution field.
  \item We prove three invariants: genesis immutability, compositional
    transitivity, and third-party verifiability.
  \item We define the \emph{Attribution DAG} and prove that it is always
    acyclic in a well-typed SAT system.
  \item We prove the \emph{Attribution Closure Theorem}: a network of agents
    operating under SAT cannot produce unattributed capabilities.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:problem} characterises the Attribution Vacuum precisely.

\section{The Attribution Vacuum}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Capabilities and Their Authors}

\begin{definition}[Capability]
  A \emph{capability} $C$ is a tuple $(I, O, f, \rho)$ where: $I$ is the input
  type, $O$ is the output type, $f : I \to O$ is the implementation, and
  $\rho$ is a resource footprint (compute, memory, latency).  A capability is
  \emph{elementary} if $f$ is not defined in terms of other capabilities; it is
  \emph{composite} if $f$ is defined by composing $k \ge 2$ other capabilities.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Author]
  The \emph{author} of a capability is the agent — human or artificial — whose
  intellectual act produced $f$.  For elementary capabilities, the author is
  the agent that wrote or generated $f$.  For composite capabilities, the
  question of authorship becomes nontrivial (Section~\ref{sec:dag}).
\end{definition}

\subsection{Why Metadata Fails}

The dominant contemporary approach is to attach authorship information as
metadata: a structured record (author name, timestamp, licence) stored
alongside the capability but not \emph{as} part of it.  This approach has four
fatal weaknesses in an autonomous AI context:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item \textbf{Separability.}  Metadata can be stripped from a capability
    without modifying the capability itself.  A downstream agent that copies $f$
    but not its metadata has the capability without the attribution.
  \item \textbf{Mutability.}  Metadata records are data.  Data can be
    overwritten.  Any agent with write access to the metadata store can alter
    or delete authorship records.
  \item \textbf{Latency.}  Metadata is typically attached after the fact —
    after the capability is created, tested, and deployed.  In a high-frequency
    autonomous agent network, the window between capability creation and metadata
    attachment is a vulnerability.
  \item \textbf{Trust dependency.}  Verifying metadata requires trusting the
    metadata store and its access control system.  In a decentralised multi-agent
    network, no single store may be trusted by all participants.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Formal Statement of the Problem}

Let $\mathcal{N}$ be a network of $N$ autonomous agents, each capable of
generating, composing, and deploying capabilities.  Let $\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{N})$
be the set of all capabilities produced by $\mathcal{N}$ over a time horizon
$T$.  Define the \emph{attribution function}
$\alpha : \mathcal{C}(\mathcal{N}) \to 2^\mathcal{A}$
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of agents and $\alpha(C)$ is the (possibly empty)
set of attributed authors.

The Attribution Vacuum problem is: under a metadata-based attribution scheme,
$\alpha(C) = \emptyset$ for a non-negligible fraction of $\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{N})$,
and $|\alpha(C)| > |\text{true authors of } C|$ for another non-negligible
fraction (false attribution by metadata injection).

\section{The Structural Attribution Type}
\label{sec:sat}

\subsection{SAT Definition}

\begin{definition}[Structural Attribution Type]
  The \emph{Structural Attribution Type} is a type constructor $\ATTR[-]$ such
  that $\ATTR[C]$ is the attributed form of capability $C$.  Formally:
  \[
    \ATTR[C] = (C,\; \LOCK(a,\, h,\, t_0,\, \sigma))
  \]
  where:
  \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item $C = (I, O, f, \rho)$ is the underlying capability (unchanged);
    \item $a \in \mathcal{A}$ is the \emph{author} — the agent that produced $f$;
    \item $h = H(C \| a \| t_0)$ is a \emph{genesis hash} — a cryptographic
      commitment to the pairing of capability content, author, and creation time;
    \item $t_0$ is the \emph{genesis timestamp};
    \item $\sigma = \text{Sign}_{k_a}(h)$ is the author's digital signature
      over the genesis hash.
  \end{itemize}
  The $\LOCK$ record is \emph{sealed}: it is computed once at genesis and has no
  setter method.  The only operation defined on $\LOCK$ is \texttt{verify},
  which checks the signature and hash.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Well-Typedness}

\begin{definition}[Well-Typed Capability Network]
  A capability network $\mathcal{N}$ is \emph{well-typed under SAT} if:
  (i) every capability produced by any agent in $\mathcal{N}$ is of type
  $\ATTR[C]$ for some $C$; and (ii) no agent in $\mathcal{N}$ can produce a
  value of type $C$ directly — only of type $\ATTR[C]$.
\end{definition}

Condition (ii) is the critical one.  It is enforced by the type checker: the
production operation $\GEN : \mathcal{A} \times I \times O \times (I \to O)
\to \ATTR[\CAP]$ is the \emph{only} way to introduce a capability into the
system, and $\GEN$ is defined to compute $\LOCK$ as its first act.

\section{Three Core Invariants}
\label{sec:invariants}

\subsection{Invariant 1: Genesis Immutability}

\begin{theorem}[Genesis Immutability]
  In a well-typed SAT system, for any capability $C \in \ATTR[\CAP]$ with
  $\LOCK = \LOCK(a, h, t_0, \sigma)$, no operation in the system can produce
  a value $C'$ such that $C'$ has the same genesis hash $h$ but a different
  author $a'$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  The genesis hash $h = H(C \| a \| t_0)$ binds the author $a$ to the
  capability content $C$ and genesis time $t_0$ through the collision-resistant
  hash $H$.  Changing $a$ to $a' \ne a$ while keeping $h$ unchanged requires
  finding a hash collision $H(C \| a \| t_0) = H(C \| a' \| t_0)$, which is
  computationally infeasible for any collision-resistant $H$.
  Changing $h$ breaks the signature verification
  $\text{Verify}_{k_a}(h, \sigma) = \top$, rendering $C'$ not well-typed.
  Therefore no well-typed operation can alter the author field. \qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Invariant 2: Compositional Transitivity}

When a composite capability $C_{12}$ is formed by composing $C_1$ and $C_2$,
the question is whether the attribution of $C_{12}$ preserves the attributions
of $C_1$ and $C_2$.

\begin{definition}[Composition Operation]
  Given $\ATTR[C_1]$ and $\ATTR[C_2]$ with $O_1 = I_2$, the composite
  $\ATTR[C_{12}] = \PROP(\ATTR[C_1], \ATTR[C_2], a_\text{composer})$ is:
  \[
    \ATTR[C_{12}] = \bigl((I_1, O_2, f_2 \circ f_1, \rho_1 + \rho_2),\;
      \LOCK(a_\text{composer}, H(C_{12} \| \{a_1, a_2\} \| t_0^\text{new}),
            t_0^\text{new}, \sigma_\text{composer})\bigr)
  \]
  where $\{a_1, a_2\}$ are the authors of $C_1$ and $C_2$, serialised
  canonically into the genesis hash of $C_{12}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Compositional Transitivity]
  In a well-typed SAT system, the genesis hash of any composite capability
  $C_{12}$ contains the authorship identifiers of all of its component
  capabilities, to arbitrary depth.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By induction on composition depth $d$.

  \medskip\noindent
  \textbf{Base case} ($d=1$): $C_{12}$ is composed of two elementary capabilities
  $C_1, C_2$.  By definition of $\PROP$, the genesis hash
  $h_{12} = H(C_{12} \| \{a_1, a_2\} \| t_0^{\text{new}})$ directly encodes
  $a_1$ and $a_2$.

  \medskip\noindent
  \textbf{Inductive step}: Assume the theorem holds for all compositions of depth
  $d-1$.  A depth-$d$ composition $C_{1\cdots k}$ is formed by composing
  $C_{1\cdots k-1}$ (depth $d-1$, with hash encoding all authors $a_1,\ldots,a_{k-1}$)
  with $C_k$ (elementary).  By $\PROP$, the hash of $C_{1\cdots k}$ encodes
  the hash of $C_{1\cdots k-1}$ as a component of its author set — which by the
  inductive hypothesis encodes $a_1, \ldots, a_{k-1}$.  Therefore the hash of
  $C_{1\cdots k}$ encodes all of $a_1, \ldots, a_k$. \qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Invariant 3: Third-Party Verifiability}

\begin{theorem}[Third-Party Verifiability]
  Any agent $a_v$ — including one that did not participate in the creation of
  $\ATTR[C]$ — can verify the attribution of $C$ without trusting any
  intermediary, using only: (i) the public key $k_a^{\text{pub}}$ of the claimed
  author, and (ii) the $\LOCK$ record.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Verification proceeds in two steps:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item Recompute $h' = H(C \| a \| t_0)$ from the capability content $C$,
      the claimed author $a$, and the genesis timestamp $t_0$ — all of which
      are fields of $\ATTR[C]$.
    \item Check $\text{Verify}_{k_a^{\text{pub}}}(h', \sigma) = \top$.
  \end{enumerate}
  If both checks pass, the attribution is verified.  The verification uses only
  public information and requires no trust in the metadata store, the network
  operator, or the author themselves.  The security of the verification reduces
  to the security of the digital signature scheme. \qed
\end{proof}

\section{The Attribution DAG}
\label{sec:dag}

\subsection{Definition}

\begin{definition}[Attribution DAG]
  The \emph{Attribution DAG} $G = (V, E)$ of a capability network $\mathcal{N}$
  is defined as:
  \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item $V = \mathcal{C}(\mathcal{N})$: one vertex per capability;
    \item $(C_i, C_j) \in E$ if and only if $C_j$ was directly composed from
      $C_i$ (i.e.\ $C_i$ is a direct component of $C_j$).
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Attribution DAG is Acyclic]
  In a well-typed SAT system, the Attribution DAG is always a directed acyclic
  graph.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Suppose for contradiction there exists a cycle
  $C_1 \to C_2 \to \cdots \to C_k \to C_1$.
  Then $C_1$ is a component of $C_k$, which is a component of $C_{k-1}$, \ldots,
  which is a component of $C_1$.  By the definition of $\PROP$, $C_1$ is
  composed from $C_k$ — meaning $C_1$ is produced \emph{after} $C_k$.
  But $C_k$ is composed from $C_1$, meaning $C_k$ is produced after $C_1$.
  Together: $t_0(C_1) > t_0(C_k)$ and $t_0(C_k) > t_0(C_1)$, a contradiction.
  Therefore no cycle exists. \qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Attribution Lineage Query}

The Attribution DAG enables efficient lineage queries.  For any capability $C$,
the set of all authors that contributed (directly or indirectly) to $C$ is:
\[
  \ATTR_*(C) = \{a : \exists\, C' \in \text{Ancestors}(C, G),\;
  C'.\LOCK.a = a\},
\]
where $\text{Ancestors}(C, G)$ is the set of all ancestors of $C$ in $G$.  This
query runs in $O(|V| + |E|)$ time by a depth-first traversal of the DAG.

\begin{proposition}[Completeness of Attribution Lineage]
  In a well-typed SAT system, $\ATTR_*(C)$ is the complete set of all agents
  whose intellectual acts contributed to $C$.  No contributing author can be
  excluded from $\ATTR_*(C)$ without breaking the well-typedness condition.
\end{proposition}

\section{Attribution Closure}
\label{sec:closure}

\begin{theorem}[Attribution Closure]
  In a well-typed SAT capability network $\mathcal{N}$, for every capability
  $C \in \mathcal{C}(\mathcal{N})$:
  \[
    \alpha(C) = \ATTR_*(C) \ne \emptyset.
  \]
  That is, every capability in $\mathcal{N}$ has a non-empty, verifiable
  attribution set.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the well-typedness condition, every capability $C$ was introduced into the
  system via the $\GEN$ operation, which computes $\LOCK(a, h, t_0, \sigma)$
  for some author $a \in \mathcal{A}$.  Therefore every elementary capability
  has $|\ATTR_*(C)| \ge 1$.  For composite capabilities, $\ATTR_*(C)$ contains
  all ancestors' authors by Compositional Transitivity
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:3}).  Since every composite capability has at least one
  elementary component (which has at least one author), $\ATTR_*(C) \ne \emptyset$
  for all $C$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[No Unattributed AI Capabilities]
  If all agents in $\mathcal{N}$ are required to produce only well-typed SAT
  capabilities, then no autonomous AI agent can produce a capability that lacks
  authorship attribution.
\end{corollary}

This corollary is the central practical result.  It means that in a network
where the SAT type discipline is enforced at the runtime level — not at the
policy level — the Attribution Vacuum cannot occur.

\section{Multi-Agent AI Networks under SAT}
\label{sec:multiagent}

\subsection{Autonomous Capability Markets}

Consider a market in which AI agents buy and sell capabilities.  Under
metadata-based attribution, a buyer cannot verify that the capability they
purchased is attributed to the seller rather than a third party whose work was
incorporated without attribution.  Under SAT, the Attribution DAG settles this
question: the buyer runs a lineage query and observes exactly which authors
contributed at each depth.

This enables a new class of \emph{attribution-aware pricing}: the market price
of a capability can reflect the number and quality of contributing authors, with
royalties routed proportionally to each author's contribution at their depth in
the DAG — automatically, at the time of sale, without human mediation.

\subsection{Attribution Routing}

\begin{definition}[Attribution-Weighted Royalty]
  For a composite capability $C$ sold at price $p$, the royalty for author $a_i$
  at depth $d_i$ in the Attribution DAG is:
  \[
    r_i = p \cdot \frac{w(d_i)}{\sum_j w(d_j)},
  \]
  where $w : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is a depth-weighting function.
  A natural choice is $w(d) = \beta^{-d}$ for $\beta > 1$: more proximate
  authors receive higher royalties.
\end{definition}

Under this scheme, the direct author of a composite capability receives the
largest royalty, and royalties decrease geometrically toward the root authors
of elementary components.  The scheme is computable from the Attribution DAG
alone and requires no human judgment.

\subsection{Agent Identity and Key Management}

SAT requires each agent to maintain a public/private key pair for signing
genesis records.  For AI agents, this raises the question of key ownership: who
controls the key that signs the agent's outputs?

We distinguish three regimes:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item \textbf{Human-controlled key}: the agent's outputs are attributed to the
    human who controls the signing key.  This is appropriate for AI-assisted
    human authorship.
  \item \textbf{Organisation-controlled key}: the agent's outputs are attributed
    to the organisation that operates it.  This is appropriate for commercial
    AI deployments.
  \item \textbf{On-chain principal}: the agent's key pair is derived from its
    on-chain identifier (e.g.\ a canister principal on a blockchain).  This is
    the fully decentralised regime, where the agent's authorship is as
    irrevocable as its on-chain identity.
\end{enumerate}

Regime (3) is most robust against the Attribution Vacuum: the agent's key
cannot be revoked without destroying the agent itself, and the attribution is
anchored to an immutable blockchain record.

\subsection{Sybil Attribution Attacks}

An adversary that controls multiple agent identities could attempt to flood the
Attribution DAG with many shallow agents, each claiming authorship of components
that they did not meaningfully author.  We call this a \emph{sybil attribution
attack}.

The depth-weighted royalty function $w(d) = \beta^{-d}$ partially mitigates
this: shallow sybil agents that contribute trivially (a composition layer with
no intellectual content) receive vanishingly small royalties as $\beta$ grows.
A more robust mitigation requires a \emph{contribution quality signal} — an
oracle that rates the intellectual content of each capability independent of its
position in the DAG.

\begin{proposition}[Sybil Resistance under Quality Gating]
  If capabilities must pass a minimum quality threshold $q_\text{min}$ to be
  included in the market (verified by a trusted oracle), then a sybil agent
  must produce capabilities of quality $\ge q_\text{min}$ to earn non-trivial
  royalties, limiting the effectiveness of sybil attribution attacks.
\end{proposition}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Software provenance.}
Provenance tracking in software supply chains~\cite{sigstore2021, in-toto2019}
records \emph{how} an artefact was produced (build steps, tools, inputs) rather
than \emph{who} produced it.  SAT is complementary: it tracks authorship, not
build process.  The two can be combined.

\paragraph{AI-generated content attribution.}
Recent proposals~\cite{bender2021, bommasani2021} address attribution of
AI-generated text and images at the policy and metadata level.  SAT provides
a complementary \emph{technical} enforcement mechanism that operates
independently of policy.

\paragraph{Blockchain-based IP.}
NFTs and on-chain copyright registries~\cite{bored2021} associate blockchain
tokens with creative works.  SAT differs in that attribution is baked into the
capability's type, not into a separate token.  The two approaches are
compatible: an NFT can represent the $\LOCK$ record of a SAT capability.

\paragraph{Type-theoretic security.}
Information-flow type systems~\cite{volpano1997, heintze1998} enforce security
policies through types.  SAT follows the same paradigm for attribution: by
making the attribution field a type invariant rather than a runtime check, it
achieves the same robustness guarantees as information-flow types.

\paragraph{Curry-Howard and specification correctness.}
The Curry-Howard isomorphism~\cite{howard1980} identifies proofs with programs.
SAT adds a third axis: attribution.  Under SAT, a capability is simultaneously
a program (its implementation), a specification (its type), and a provenance
record (its attribution).  This three-way identification is consistent with the
Curry-Howard tradition.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Computational Overhead}

SAT adds a constant-size $\LOCK$ record to every capability.  The genesis
hash and signature are computed once, at capability creation, and verified on
demand.  Verification costs one hash computation and one signature check —
$O(1)$ in capability size for most hash and signature schemes.  The Attribution
DAG has $|V| = |\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{N})|$ vertices and $|E| \le |V|^2$ edges
(bounded by the number of compositions), making lineage queries $O(|V| + |E|)$.

\subsection{What SAT Does Not Solve}

SAT guarantees that the attribution in a capability's $\LOCK$ record is
\emph{internally consistent} and \emph{verifiable}.  It does not guarantee
that the attributed author is the \emph{true} intellectual originator of the
capability: an agent could use a stolen key, or could plagiarise the
implementation $f$ from an unattributed source.  SAT makes attribution
\emph{structurally irrevocable}; it cannot make it \emph{ethically correct}
without additional oracle mechanisms.

\subsection{Key Compromise}

If an agent's private key is compromised, an adversary can forge attributions
signed by that key.  Standard key rotation mechanisms partially address this,
but rotating a key changes the attribution identity of future capabilities.
A key revocation list that marks compromised keys would allow verifiers to
reject attributions signed after the compromise date.

\subsection{Implications for AI Governance}

SAT has implications beyond intellectual property.  If a capability with harmful
outputs has a traceable Attribution DAG, regulators can identify the responsible
agents at each depth and hold them accountable proportionally.  This provides a
technical foundation for \emph{tiered AI liability}: the author of an elementary
component is less liable than the composer of the capability that caused harm,
but neither is entirely off the hook — and both are identifiable.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The Attribution Vacuum — the growing space of AI-generated capabilities without
verifiable authorship — is a structural problem that requires a structural
solution.  We have shown that treating authorship as a type-system invariant
rather than a metadata overlay achieves Attribution Closure: in a well-typed
SAT network, no capability can exist without a verifiable, irrevocable
authorship record.

The three invariants — genesis immutability, compositional transitivity, and
third-party verifiability — together ensure that the attribution record is
tamper-proof, complete, and trustless.  The Attribution DAG provides an
efficient data structure for lineage queries and attribution-weighted royalty
routing in autonomous capability markets.

As AI agents grow in autonomy, capability, and economic significance, the
question of who created what will become one of the defining legal and technical
challenges of the decade.  Structural attribution offers a path in which that
question has a definitive, machine-verifiable answer — embedded in the fabric
of the capability itself.
